Where it comes from

Five levers, each with the arithmetic showing. No black box.

1 · Recency lift

$0

Every point of recency is one percent of the book paying one more month. This is the biggest lever on the page and the one the desk works on directly — the Pulse screen lists exactly which accounts haven't paid in 30 days, sorted, every morning. Read this one as gross cash flow, not profit — some of these accounts would have paid eventually on their own, and some were going to charge off no matter who called. It is the ceiling on the lever, not a promise.

2 · Payments that reach principal

$0

On a simple-interest note, a late payment can be all interest and no principal. The customer pays, the balance doesn't move, and the note runs longer than the car lasts. The desk flags it mid-call and tells the collector the smallest amount that actually reduces the balance.

3 · Repos you don't have to make

$0

This one is smaller for you than for most lots, and it should be. When a unit comes back you tow it yourself, recon it, and sell it again with a fresh down payment — you are not dumping it at auction for thirty cents. So a repo isn't a catastrophe here. It's a cost: your tow, your recon, your lot space, and the gap between what was owed and what it's worth the second time.

4 · Getting there faster

$0

This is the lever the wrecker creates. When executing is cheap, the expensive thing is the gap between "we should take this one" and someone actually going. Every day in between is depreciation, risk of damage, and a unit that isn't back on the line earning a down payment. The 30-day skip board, the GPS log and the cure-date alarms exist to shrink exactly that gap.

5 · Compliance exposure avoided

$0

TCPA damages run $500–$1,500 per message, and they multiply. The desk blocks a text to anyone who opted out or whose consent went stale, blocks calls past the frequency cap, and locks out contact after office hours. One avoided incident, spread across a year.

What it doesn't do

The part nobody puts on a comparison chart. Read this before deciding anything.

  • It isn't a DMS. No inventory, no deal desking, no title work, no forms. The DMS stays the book of record.
  • It doesn't sync with anything. Aging comes in by CSV and goes back out by CSV. Payments get posted twice — here and in the DMS.
  • Data lives on one device. What Maricela enters doesn't appear on the other collector's phone. One shared board needs a server.
  • It can't send a text or take a payment itself. It writes the message and hands you the link. Your phone sends it; Authorize.net takes the money.
  • It doesn't write legal language. Your attorney's notice goes in once and the app merges names and dates into it. That division is deliberate.
  • Nobody supports it but us. Frazer answers the phone at 8am. That is worth real money and this has no equivalent.

Where the figures come from

  1. Recency target of low-to-mid 90s and the collected-vs-due framing: BHPH portfolio management guidance published in Auto Finance News.
  2. Early vs. late-stage recovery (roughly 85% early, 11% past 180 days): ACA International industry data.
  3. Auction recovery near 30 cents on the dollar is the industry figure (Recovery Database Network, Q3 2025) and is deliberately not used here — a lot that tows and re-retails its own units recovers far more than that, which is why lever 3 is modeled on re-retail value instead.
  4. Depreciation while a unit is out is set at $12/day, a placeholder on a high-mileage vehicle of unknown condition. Change it if you have a better number.
  5. TCPA damages of $500–$1,500 per message: statutory range widely reported in collections compliance guidance.
  6. Competitor pricing as publicly advertised in 2025–26 software directories. Prices change; confirm before comparing.
  7. Feature marks reflect what each vendor documents publicly as of August 2026. Absence of documentation isn't proof of absence.
